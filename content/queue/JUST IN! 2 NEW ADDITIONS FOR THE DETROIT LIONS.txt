Start with the name that made every long-time Lions fan's chest tighten a little this week: Taylor Decker. Because months after one of the strangest breakups this franchise has had, the big man is still out there, unsigned, waiting for a call. And for a guy who gave this organization ten full years, that's a genuinely strange sentence to say out loud in October.

Let's give the career the respect it deserves, because younger fans might not fully appreciate what Decker was here. Detroit took him in the first round of the 2016 draft, 16th overall, and he walked straight into the starting left tackle job as a rookie. Not eased in. Not redshirted. Started all 16 games and played essentially every single offensive snap of his rookie year, which earned him PFWA All-Rookie honors. A shoulder injury bit into his second season, and that 2017 year was really the only serious dent in an otherwise remarkable run of availability. He came back, planted his flag at left tackle, and never let go of it. Think about what that continuity meant: Decker protected the blind side through the dark years, through the rebuild, through the Campbell culture reset, all the way into the era where this team started hosting playoff games. Quarterbacks came and went, coordinators came and went, and the left tackle stayed the same. In a league where offensive line continuity is half the battle, that's not a small legacy. That's a decade of someone else's problems never becoming Detroit's problem. In 2020 the Lions paid him like the cornerstone he was: a five-year extension worth 70.35 million dollars. The final ledger in Detroit reads 145 games, every single one of them a start, three playoff appearances, two division titles, and more Thanksgiving afternoons in that uniform than almost anyone on those rosters. And it wasn't just the football. Decker was woven into this city, the community work, the relationship with this fan base. He suffered through the 0-16-adjacent years and survived long enough to play meaningful January football here. That matters.

Which is what made March so jarring. Decker told the organization he wanted to come back. The two sides sat down, and they simply could not find a number that worked. So the player himself requested his release, and the Lions granted it, ten years, done, just like that. No farewell season, no ring-of-honor sendoff, just a transaction line in March. And here's the kicker: months later, the market has not treated him the way many expected. He remains unsigned, still waiting on the next opportunity, and some of the national coverage has been blunt about it, suggesting he misread what the league was willing to pay at this stage of his career. From a human standpoint, that stings to watch.

Now, the obvious question this fan base keeps asking: should Detroit be the team that calls? Here's the honest Real Lions Here answer, and it's layered. The romance says yes. The roster says: look closer. Decker's departure is the entire reason this line looks the way it does, it's why Penei Sewell, an All-World right tackle, flipped over to the left side this season, and why rookie first-rounder Blake Miller owns the right tackle job. That transition has had wobbles, but it's the plan, and the plan is working well enough that this offense has scored 30-plus in every game. Bringing Decker back isn't plugging a hole, it's reopening a settled question, and he's been out of football since March, which means conditioning and timing are real concerns. BUT, and underline this, we are one tackle injury away from that math flipping completely. If Sewell or Miller misses time, a ten-year franchise left tackle sitting by his phone suddenly becomes the most obvious emergency call in football. File it away. October says no. December might say something very different.

Story two, and this one actually turned into news while the week unfolded: the Lions brought wide receiver Lawrence Keys III back in for a workout. And if that name rings a bell, it should, because Keys was in this building just a few months ago. Detroit released him on August 5th, during the first week of training camp, through an injury settlement after a hamstring issue. Bringing a guy back for a workout less than two months later tells you the team wanted a close look at how his rehab went, and spoiler for the end of this segment: they liked what they saw.

Why the interest? One word: speed. Real, measurable, scary speed. Keys has been clocked around 4.35 in the 40, roughly 10.3 in the 100 meters, and his game is built on exactly what those numbers suggest: instant acceleration, attacking open grass on routes, and, critically for this roster, punt return ability. His road here is the definition of earning it: Notre Dame first, then a transfer to Tulane, where he put up 63 catches, 895 yards and nine touchdowns across 23 games with the Green Wave, including a final season of 33 catches for 599 yards and seven scores, an absurd 18.2 yards per catch. Then the professional grind: time with the Buffalo Bills organization, a detour through the Houston Gamblers in spring football where he balled out, 24 catches, 349 yards, two touchdowns, plus over 500 kickoff return yards and change on punts, and then the June deal with Detroit that the hamstring cut short.

And here's why this matters more than a typical camp-body workout. Count the receivers on this active roster: Amon-Ra St. Brown, Jameson Williams, Isaac TeSlaa, Tay Martin. Four. That's it, the leanest receiver room in football, and we've talked for weeks about the punt returner hole this team has been living with since Greg Dortch was cut loose and landed in Buffalo. A returner with legitimate track speed who already knows this building, this staff and this system is exactly the profile you stash for a December moment. Because look at how clean the role division is in that receiver room right now: St. Brown owns the middle of the field and the dirty work, Jamo stretches the roof off the defense, TeSlaa brings the size and contested catches, and Tay Martin earns his helmet on special teams. What's missing from that quartet is a pure return-game gadget, a guy whose entire job description is making the first man miss with the ball in space. An NFL team can always find room for more speed, and it can especially find room for speed that doesn't need a playbook orientation. And sure enough, the workout wasn't just a look: Detroit signed Keys to the practice squad the very next day. Speed is back in the pipeline. While they were at it, the Lions also promoted linebacker Amen Ogbongbemiga from the practice squad to the active roster, a 28-year-old veteran of 73 NFL games who already saw the field in the Jets win, more special teams muscle, more depth insurance. Quiet moves. Smart moves. The kind this front office keeps stacking.

Alright. Deep breath, because now we get to the main event: Joey Porter Jr., and the three trade scenarios that have this fan base arguing in every comment section I can find. The background, if you're just joining this saga: Porter still has not played a regular season snap this year, tangled in what has become a full-blown contract standoff in Pittsburgh while other members of his 2023 draft class cash major extensions around him. A talented, physical, man-coverage corner sitting in the window while Detroit's secondary deals with everything we described at the top? Of course the speculation found us. We've been covering this team's cornerback problem since the 621 passing yards in two weeks, since the September DB workouts, since the Kenny Moore check-in. The need is real. The question has always been the price. And understand why Porter specifically keeps gravitating toward Detroit in these conversations: this is a long, physical, press-man corner, the exact archetype Kelvin Sheppard's defense is begging for. Everything we've watched this unit try to become, the layered pressures, the zone blitzes, the disguise-heavy looks, works twice as well when your corners can survive on an island for the extra half-second it takes the pressure to get home. Right now, with the injury list where it is, Detroit's corners are surviving on effort and scheme help. Porter is the kind of piece that flips that equation. And this week CBS Sports laid out three different versions of what that price could look like. Let's grade them one by one.

Scenario one: Detroit sends its 2027 second-round pick and a 2028 third-rounder to Pittsburgh, and Joey Porter Jr. comes back alone. Of the three, this is the deal that best protects the Lions' most important future assets, because the first-round pick stays home. Make no mistake, a two and a three is still a serious investment, but here's why protecting that first matters so much: the bills are coming due in Detroit. Sam LaPorta and Brian Branch are both 2023 draft picks, both are going to want significant money soon, and this franchise has to manage its cap and its draft pipeline with surgical care to keep this window open. A deal built on Day 2 and Day 3 capital threads that needle. The catch? If Omar Khan's asking price is genuinely high, this package probably gets laughed off the phone. And one more flashing light: if Porter arrives without an extension, you might be renting a player for a few months at the cost of two real picks. My grade: the only version I'd seriously consider, and even then, only with extension talks already warm. Because here's the cold rental math nobody likes saying out loud: if he walks after the season, your second-round pick bought you three months of cornerback play and, at best, a compensatory crumb down the road. Front offices that win consistently simply don't make that exchange without a plan for the years after.

Scenario two: Detroit sends its 2027 FIRST-round pick plus a sixth, and gets Porter and a 2027 third back. Folks, no. I understand the third-rounder coming back softens the math, but a first-round pick is the single most valuable asset a well-run franchise owns, and this particular franchise built its entire contender core, Sewell, LaPorta, Gibbs, Branch, Campbell, on nailing those premium picks. You do not ship one out for a cornerback who is currently in a contract staring contest with his own team, unless you are absolutely certain he signs long-term in Detroit the moment the trade clears. With that certainty, this becomes defensible, you're buying years of a 24-year-old man corner, not months. Without it, it's a panic move wearing a trade's clothing. And nothing about Brad Holmes' track record says panic.

And then there's scenario three, the nuclear one, and if you watched our video last week you already know where I stand: Detroit receives Porter, DK Metcalf and a 2027 fourth-rounder, Pittsburgh receives Jameson Williams AND the 2027 first. This is the full roster-reshaping fantasy, two areas addressed in one swing, and I'm telling you again: hard pass. Jameson Williams is an explosive-play machine who just started heating up, and this franchise spent years developing him into exactly what he is now. Metcalf is older, was productive once but has struggled in Pittsburgh, and there's the history nobody in Detroit has forgotten: the altercation with a fan at Ford Field that ended with a suspension and a legal mess that eventually fizzled out. You want to put that jersey on and sell it on Gratiot Avenue? Trading a 25-year-old homegrown deep threat plus a first for an aging receiver with baggage and a corner mid-standoff isn't aggressive team-building. It's collecting other people's problems and paying premium prices for the privilege.

So where does the whole Porter saga actually land? Exactly where it's been all along: this is a price conversation, not a talent conversation. Nobody disputes that a healthy, motivated Joey Porter Jr. makes this secondary better, especially with Branch and Joseph still working back and the depth chart held together by Chuck Clark, Christian Izien and effort. But Brad Holmes has built this entire era by refusing to pay desperation prices, and a team that's 2-1 with the league's hottest offense does not need to light its future on fire in the first week of October. If Pittsburgh's price drops into that scenario-one range, make the call. If it doesn't, trust the cavalry that's already coming: Branch is eligible to return soon, Joseph is rehabbing, Rakestraw is growing up in real time, and the trade deadline is still weeks away. Patience is a weapon too. And remember what's already on the calendar: the bye week sits out there as the natural reset point, Cade Mays is targeting it for his return to fortify the interior, Branch's window opens around week five, and if even two of those dominoes fall right, the roster that comes out of the bye looks meaningfully stronger than the one playing tomorrow, without spending a single draft pick.
