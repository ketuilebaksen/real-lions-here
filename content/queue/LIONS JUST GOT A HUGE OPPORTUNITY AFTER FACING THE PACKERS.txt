The Packers chapter is closed, and before the dust has even settled at Ford Field, the next battle is already on the clock: November 10th, the NFL trade deadline, is two weeks away. Eight defensive backs are being connected to Detroit, five cornerbacks and three safeties, including an eight-time Pro Bowler who just sacked Jared Goff a few weeks ago, a Super Bowl champion corner stuck on a sinking team, and a 27-interception ballhawk on an expiring deal. One of these moves could completely change the outlook of this secondary. And on top of all that, the Lions already made an official move for a familiar face that quietly matters more than it looks.

First, the frame, because every trade conversation only makes sense inside it. Detroit came out of the bye at 3-2, just finished the first of two straight home division fights, Green Bay now, Minnesota right behind them, and the mission is written on the wall in Allen Park: a third NFC North title in four years. After missing the playoffs last season, nobody in that building is pretending this is a rebuild or a retool. This is a contender with one visible crack, and everybody in the league knows where it is: the secondary. We’ve documented the whole saga on this channel since September, the 621 passing yards in two weeks, the DB workouts, the Joey Porter Jr. scenarios, Kerby Joseph still out with no timeline, the parade of injured safeties. Brian Branch coming back changes the ceiling, but it doesn’t change the question Brad Holmes has to answer in the next two weeks: do you trust this roster as-is, or do you spend assets to reinforce it? And once these two division games are played, he’ll know exactly where this defense stands with the receipts to prove it.

Enter Sports Illustrated’s Christian Booher, who put together a big board of eight defensive backs who could make sense for Detroit before November 10th: five corners, three safeties. Important disclaimer before the fun starts: these are potential targets and fits, not confirmed negotiations, nobody is reporting active talks. But every name on this list is on it for a reason, so let’s go one by one, cornerbacks first.

Name one, and it’s a spicy one: Riq Woolen, now of the Philadelphia Eagles. Yes, that Riq Woolen, the long, absurdly fast corner who started his career in Seattle and helped the Seahawks win the Super Bowl last season. He signed a one-year deal in Philadelphia this offseason, and here’s why he might actually shake loose: the Eagles are 2-4, Jalen Hurts is on injured reserve, and if that season keeps sliding, Philadelphia becomes a seller with a stack of expiring contracts to flip. Why do I like this one for Detroit? Three reasons. One, the physical profile is exactly what Kelvin Sheppard’s scheme is starving for: rare length, rare speed, a corner who can live on an island while the pressure packages do their work. Two, the one-year contract means Detroit isn’t marrying anybody, this is a rental with no long-term cap entanglement, and if it works, you negotiate from the inside track. Three, the price for half a season of a rental corner, even a Super Bowl champion one, should stay in the Day 3 pick neighborhood, not the premium-asset neighborhood. The risk? Rentals walk, and consistency has never been Woolen’s middle name. But as a calculated, low-commitment strike, this is my favorite kind of deadline move.

Name two is an old friend of this channel’s trade board: Kristian Fulton of the Kansas City Chiefs. We first broke him down back in September, and the logic has only gotten stronger since. Kansas City is sitting there undefeated with a genuine logjam at corner, L’Jarius Sneed, first-round pick Mansoor Delane, the kids coming on, and Fulton on the final year of his deal watching the rotation tighten around him. Undefeated teams don’t usually sell, but this wouldn’t be selling, it would be converting a luxury spare part into draft capital, which is exactly the kind of deal contenders make with each other. For Detroit, Fulton isn’t a transformation, let’s be honest about that. He’s a stabilizer, an experienced starter-level corner to slot in across from D.J. Reed and raise the floor of every coverage call. Sometimes a contender doesn’t need the splash. It needs one fewer position to hold its breath about. Cheap, expiring, proven: Fulton checks the boxes.

Name three is where the price of poker goes up: Zyon McCollum of the Tampa Bay Buccaneers. The talent argument is real, McCollum has legitimate man-coverage chops and the Bucs’ 1-5 freefall could absolutely put veterans on the market. But here’s where I pump the brakes hard: the contract. McCollum signed a three-year, $48 million extension that keeps him under contract through 2028. That’s not a rental, that’s a financial commitment stapled to a trade, and remember whose money is already earmarked in Detroit: Sam LaPorta and Brian Branch, the crown jewels of the 2023 class, are both lining up for major extensions. Brad Holmes has built this entire era on sequencing those payments correctly. Giving up real draft picks AND absorbing $16 million a year of cornerback money? Unless Tampa Bay’s asking price is shockingly reasonable, I’m out. Talent says yes. The books say be careful.

Name four: Donte Jackson of the Los Angeles Chargers. The situation screams availability, the Chargers have lost six straight to start the season, the fire sale alarm is practically ringing in the building, and Jackson is on the final year of his contract. He’s a true veteran who has played in multiple defensive systems, which usually means a fast adjustment to a new playbook, and speed has always been his calling card. But here’s my one flashing caution light: he’s missed the last two games with a hamstring injury, and when you’re trading for IMMEDIATE defensive help in late October, availability isn’t a detail, it’s the entire point. You cannot spend a pick on a corner who spends November on the training table. If the medicals check out, fine, he’s a sensible depth add at a bargain price. And file this footnote away: Booher notes Detroit could also sniff around the Chargers’ younger backs, Tarheeb Still and Cam Hart, though prying young cost-controlled players out of even a bad team costs real money. Jackson is the realistic one.

And name five, the lottery ticket: Kelee Ringo of the Eagles. Different shape of move entirely. Ringo entered the 2023 draft as a famous name with first-round buzz and elite physical tools, slid on draft day, and has simply never carved out a defensive role in Philadelphia, this season he’s been almost exclusively a special teams player. So why bother? Because this is the definition of a buy-low: a 24-year-old with traits you cannot teach, available for next to nothing, from a team with no room for him. If this coaching staff believes it can develop him, and developing defensive backs is literally what this regime has had to do all season, Ringo could be found money. But let’s be clear-eyed: he is not walking in and fixing anything in November. He’s a dice roll for December and beyond. Depth with upside. Price it accordingly.

Okay, now the safeties, and this is where the conversation gets genuinely exciting. Because the first name is one every Lions fan watched torment us in person: Budda Baker of the Arizona Cardinals. You remember. In that 45-21 win over Arizona, Baker timed up a blitz perfectly and planted Jared Goff into the turf, a one-play demonstration of everything he still is at this stage of his career. The resume barely needs reading: eight Pro Bowls, two First Team All-Pro nods, one of the most respected tone-setters of his generation. He hits, he blitzes, he tackles in space, he leads, and critically, he’s under contract through next season, so this isn’t a half-year rental, it’s a two-playoff-run acquisition. With the Kerby Joseph situation still wrapped in uncertainty, pairing Baker’s downhill violence with Branch’s hybrid chaos is the kind of safety duo that changes a defense’s entire personality. The obstacles are obvious: Arizona has to actually be willing, and a player of this stature costs real assets. But if Brad Holmes wants ONE aggressive swing before November 10th, this is the one that makes my heart beat faster. Baker is the dream scenario on this list.

The second safety: Jessie Bates of the Atlanta Falcons, and the pitch here is one beautiful word, takeaways. Twenty-seven career interceptions. A genuine, proven, quarterback-reading ballhawk, the exact species of player this defense has been missing on the back end, even if, fair warning, he hasn’t recorded one yet this season. The situation: Atlanta is 2-4 but still breathing in a soft NFC South, so there’s zero guarantee they sell, but Bates is on the final year of his contract, and if the Falcons decide future draft capital beats a long-shot division push, the phone lines open. Think about what his skill set means for the schedule in front of us: division games against Green Bay and Minnesota, quarterbacks who will test the deep middle, and a free safety whose entire brand is baiting exactly those throws. Personally? If Atlanta picks up the phone, Bates deserves dead-serious consideration. Expiring deal, elite ball skills, severe positional need. That math works.

And the third safety, the practical one: Amani Hooker of the Tennessee Titans. Tennessee is 1-5 in Robert Saleh’s first season as head coach, and teams in that position start thinking about next year early. Hooker signed an extension before last season that runs through 2028, so like McCollum, this is a commitment trade, not a rental, but the tone is different: Hooker isn’t a headline, he’s a stabilizer, experienced, smart, reliable, the kind of safety who lines everybody up correctly and doesn’t hand out free touchdowns. He won’t generate the excitement of a Baker or a Bates. But sometimes a contender doesn’t need fireworks, it needs 65 snaps of professional football every Sunday. If the big swings fail, Hooker is a very respectable plan C.

So here’s my deadline strategy, Lions Nation, and I’ll say it plainly. The manageable corner moves are Woolen and Fulton, expiring deals, modest prices, immediate floor-raisers, do one of those almost automatically. The difference-making safety swings are Baker and Bates, and if the price is a Day 2 pick without mortgaging the first-rounder, I’m picking up the phone today. The buy-low flier is Ringo, fine at the right price, never as the only move. And the two commitment contracts, McCollum and Hooker, only work if the asking price collapses, because LaPorta and Branch’s extensions come first, always. But hear the other half of this too: Detroit should NOT make a trade just because a deadline exists. This team has core players to extend, young backs like Abney and Rakestraw actually developing in live reps, and a roster built to compete past January. We learned this exact lesson in the Porter saga: it’s a price conversation, not a talent conversation. Holmes has earned our trust on that math, and these two division games will tell him precisely how urgent the math is.

Now story two, the move that already happened, and the little arc behind it is worth telling properly. When Tom Kennedy’s knee gave out in the Arizona win, season over, this team lost one of the quietest weapons it had, a returner averaging better than 13 yards a punt runback, top six in the entire league. The first response came fast: Detroit signed veteran DeAndre Carter to the practice squad. And the resume tells you why him. Carter has been surviving in this league since 2015, when he entered as an undrafted free agent with the Baltimore Ravens, and the journey since reads like a league map: the Eagles, the Texans, the Bears, the Commanders, the Raiders, the Chargers, the Browns. There’s even a strange little Detroit thread in there: last season with Cleveland, Carter got hurt in a week four game against these very Lions. Now the same organization is his lifeline.

The career numbers are exactly what you want from a specialist: 117 catches for 1,331 receiving yards, nearly 5,000 combined punt and kickoff return yards, seven total touchdowns, and a signature moment, a 101-yard kickoff return touchdown for Washington in 2021 that won him NFC Special Teams Player of the Week. That’s a decade of handling the football in the one phase where a single mistake flips an entire game. And here’s the update that makes this section land differently than when the news first broke: Carter didn’t stay on the practice squad long. As we covered in our last video, Detroit promoted him to the 53 three days before the Packers game, and Dave Fipp handed him BOTH jobs, punt returns and kick returns, immediately. The tryout lasted a week. The job is his. Which tells you everything about how the staff graded those practice squad reps, and it means Amon-Ra St. Brown never has to stand under another punt, which might be the most important sentence in this entire video. Does Carter fully replace Kennedy’s production? That bar is high, 13-plus a return is elite territory. But a proven professional protecting possession and occasionally flipping a field beats your franchise receiver playing kamikaze, one hundred times out of one hundred. With Minnesota up next and the deadline looming, hidden yards are real currency.
